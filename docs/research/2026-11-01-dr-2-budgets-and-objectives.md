# Deep research report: hard budgets, inventories, risk, and “realism” in undiscounted stochastic shortest-path planning

## Executive summary

The planner described in the task is best treated as a **layered SSP/CMDP system rather than as one monolithic formulation**. The central distinction is between **pathwise hard limits**—“I own 80,000 A, 30,000 B, and may never exceed 500 money”—and **constraints only in expectation**. Classical constrained MDPs (CMDPs) and occupation-measure LPs handle the latter naturally, but they do **not** enforce a per-run inventory ceiling; exact hard inventories require either budget/resource augmentation or an equivalent cost-bounded reachability formulation. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn707137search0turn458319search2

My recommended semantics are:

1. Keep **expected price minimization** as the ordinary objective whenever the goal is reachable almost surely under the configured hard limits.
2. If the hard limits make almost-sure success impossible, do **not** minimize unconditional expected price alone, because “give up cheaply” can become artificially attractive. Instead compute maximal success probability \(P^\*\), then either use the lexicographic **MaxProb → minimum cost** criterion, or require a user-specified success floor \(q\le P^\*\) and minimize cost subject to it. The MCMP literature formalizes the first choice. citeturn707137search6turn687284search1
3. Represent genuinely hard inventories, money caps, and hard operation-count caps as resources in the state, but augment only the resources that must be pathwise exact. Expected time/effort or expected counts are better handled by CMDP occupation measures or Lagrange multipliers. citeturn458319search0turn707137search2
4. Exploit the fact that resource consumption creates a **monotone budget dimension**: transitions that consume resources go only to lower-budget slices. This fits naturally around the existing SCC solver: SCCs can only remain inside slices connected by zero-consumption transitions. This is an algorithmic consequence of the augmented-state construction used for cost-bounded reachability. citeturn458319search0
5. Use **CVaR/VaR optimization as an optional expensive mode**, not as the core solver. Exact infinite-horizon CVaR-SSP algorithms exist and can be practical on some \(10^5\)-state sparse models, but their worst-case complexity is exponential and optimal CVaR policies can require exponential memory. Published experiments reach 138,130 states and 304,826 transitions, but do not establish scalability to \(10^6\) states or hundreds of millions of transitions. citeturn213527view0turn649738view0
6. “Realism” should come from explicit resources—time, clicks, cognitive effort, maximum operations—or from restricting the policy class with an explicit certified loss, rather than from a hidden cutoff such as “never repeat 100,000 times.” Multi-objective MDP theory supports Pareto filtering; restricting to pure or bounded-memory policies is principled but can make feasibility NP-complete even for two expected-reward objectives, so simplicity has a real computational price. citeturn291562search0

The most important implementation implication is that the current analytical elimination of a near-1 self-loop as a macro-action of cost \(c/(1-p)\) is **not valid unchanged when each failed repetition consumes a tracked hard inventory or budget**: after each failure the augmented state is different. The exact recurrence should then proceed across remaining-budget layers, or a “repeat until success/exhaustion” macro-action may be introduced only if committing to repetition is itself an allowed policy decision. This follows directly from treating remaining resources as state. citeturn458319search0

A hard inventory of 80,000 is therefore not intrinsically problematic if it is the **only** active discrete resource and the base state space is small; the naïve multiplier is roughly 80,001. Two counters of 80,000 and 30,000 already imply about \(2.4\times10^9\) budget pairs before multiplying by the crafting state space, so full Cartesian augmentation is unacceptable. Exact implementations need sparse reachable-budget representations, dominance, resource decomposition, or a proof that some counters are irrelevant to the requested guarantee. The multi-cost bounded-reachability literature explicitly organizes computation by cost “epochs” rather than materializing a dense Cartesian product, but does not eliminate worst-case high-dimensional growth. citeturn458319search0

There is a useful monotonicity property but **no general concavity guarantee** worth building correctness around. Success probability \(P^\*(b)\) is monotone in each hard budget by feasible-set inclusion, but it need not be concave or convex: a trivial task that requires exactly two units has values \(P^\*(0)=P^\*(1)=0\) and \(P^\*(2)=P^\*(3)=1\), whose discrete increments are \(0,1,0\). Thus interpolation, coarse gridding, or skipping budget levels requires a separately proved error bound; monotonicity alone permits binary search for threshold questions but not exact interpolation. Cost-bounded reachability algorithms rely on monotonicity/dominance rather than a universal concavity theorem. citeturn458319search0

## Hard inventories, success probability, and exact policy evaluation

With a hard inventory, the most meaningful primary quantities are **success probability** and a clearly defined cost conditionality. If the policy succeeds almost surely, ordinary expected total price is unambiguous. If failure is possible, reporting only expected spend is misleading because a policy that fails early can be cheap. MCMP was developed precisely to distinguish success probability from cost-to-goal and to choose the cheapest policy among maximum-success policies. citeturn707137search6

For a fixed policy on a finite resource-augmented model, all of the basic quantities can be computed exactly up to numerical linear-system error. Let \(Q\) be the transient-state transition matrix, let \(r\) be one-step probability of entering a goal, and let \(f\) be one-step probability of entering failure/exhaustion. The eventual success vector satisfies
\[
h=r+Qh,
\]
so \(h=(I-Q)^{-1}r\) whenever the induced absorbing chain is transient. Expected total cost to absorption similarly satisfies
\[
v=c+Qv.
\]
These are standard absorbing-chain/SSP policy-evaluation equations. citeturn448697search4

The **expected total cost conditional on eventual success** can also be obtained without Monte Carlo. If \(g(s)=\mathbb E[C_{\rm total}\mathbf 1_{\{\rm success\}}\mid s]\), then for deterministic immediate cost \(c(s)\),
\[
g(s)=c(s)h(s)+\sum_{s'}Q(s,s')g(s'),
\qquad
\mathbb E[C_{\rm total}\mid {\rm success},s]=\frac{g(s)}{h(s)}
\]
when \(h(s)>0\). For finite-memory policies one first incorporates policy memory into the state. This is the same conditioning principle underlying minimum-cost-given-max-probability criteria. citeturn707137search6

I therefore recommend displaying **both** \(P(\text{success within configured limits})\) and cost statistics. For a policy with non-unit success probability, report at minimum: success probability, unconditional expected spend until goal/failure, and expected spend conditional on success. A “p90 price” should state explicitly whether the distribution includes failed runs, assigns failure \(+\infty\), or is conditioned on success; these definitions answer different questions. The dead-end SSP literature separates these semantics because assigning an arbitrary finite dead-end penalty changes the optimization criterion. citeturn687284search6turn707137search6

For optimization, the clean hierarchy is: **first test almost-sure feasibility under the hard caps**; if feasible, solve minimum expected price on that feasible model. If infeasible, compute \(P^\*\). Then either optimize cost lexicographically among \(P^\*\)-policies, or impose the user’s success floor \(q\) and optimize cost subject to \(h(s_0)\ge q\). Multi-objective reachability/CMDP methods support the latter form, although randomization can be needed when a secondary expected criterion is constrained. citeturn458319search6turn156635search5

## Risk objectives and Pareto strategies

Exact optimization of **CVaR for infinite-horizon undiscounted SSPs is known**. Meggendorfer gives both LP- and value-iteration-based algorithms that are provably correct, and shows that optimal policies can require exponential memory. The algorithmic worst case is exponential because the relevant VaR can itself be exponentially large; the paper conjectures EXPTIME-completeness but does **not** prove it. This is important to label as conjecture, not theorem. citeturn213527view0turn707583view1

Published measurements are encouraging but also show that model structure dominates raw state count. On consumer hardware with 10 GB JVM memory, the CVaR value-iteration prototype solved FireWire with 138,130 states and 304,826 transitions in about 3 seconds, while its LP version ran out of memory; WLAN had 87,345 states and 177,639 transitions and VI took about 1 second. Conversely, a 12,796-state Grid instance with 181,996 transitions took 221 seconds under VI and over an hour with LP. Those results are for sparse models and are not evidence that exact CVaR optimization will scale to the crafting case’s \(5\times10^8\) transitions. I found no published exact CVaR-SSP result demonstrating \(10^6\) states or hundreds of millions of explicit transitions. citeturn649738view0

VaR/p90 optimization is generally no easier: it is a percentile/cost-bounded reachability objective and naturally depends on cumulative cost or horizon. Multi-dimensional percentile MDPs have exact synthesis algorithms but require pseudo-polynomial or worse dependence on numeric thresholds in several important cases; general policies may need memory and randomization. citeturn156635academia9turn156635search5

For your planner, I would therefore separate **risk evaluation** from **risk optimization**. Once a candidate fixed policy is available, its cost distribution, p50, p90, and CVaR can be evaluated by dynamic programming/absorbing-chain methods much more easily than globally optimizing those quantities. Global CVaR optimization should be an opt-in mode run only after the state/action set has already been aggressively reduced by exact bounds and dominance. This distinction is consistent with the exponential complexity and published experiment sizes of exact CVaR-SSP. citeturn448697search0turn649738view0

For a mean–risk Pareto set, do not rely solely on weighted sums: nonconvex attainable sets can make a scalar weight miss non-supported Pareto points. A safer procedure is an **\(\varepsilon\)-constraint sweep**: minimize expected price subject to CVaR or p90 bounds, or minimize risk subject to a mean bound, and retain only certified non-dominated solutions. For objectives that are all expected cumulative rewards/costs, occupation-measure LPs give a convex multi-objective formulation and Pareto trade-offs can be treated by multi-objective LP/model checking; once VaR/CVaR enters, the path-distribution structure makes the problem substantially harder. citeturn458319search6turn291562search0turn448697search0

A good user-facing result is therefore a **small Pareto menu**, not hundreds of policies: for example “cheapest,” “lowest p90,” “lowest CVaR\(_{10\%}\),” and one or two knee points, after exact dominance filtering. Restricting policies to pure or bounded-memory strategies is a legitimate “simplicity” knob, but checking multi-objective achievability for pure stationary policies is already NP-complete for two expected-reward objectives, so simplicity should be represented as an explicit model restriction and its optimality gap should be reported where it can be bounded. citeturn291562search0

## Exact pruning, large inventories, and realism

User limits can provide **strong exact pruning** before full graph expansion. If a relaxation that ignores some restrictions gives an upper bound \(U(s,b)\) on attainable success probability and \(U(s,b)<q\), then state/branch \((s,b)\) can be discarded without losing any policy satisfying the success requirement. Likewise, if a lower bound on a required resource exceeds the remaining budget, the state is infeasible. These are ordinary admissible-bound arguments; cost-bounded reachability algorithms use analogous epoch bounds and dominance. citeturn458319search0

Occupation-measure LPs provide another useful certificate. For an operation \(i\), minimize its expected number of uses over all policies satisfying the required reachability/properness constraints. If that lower bound exceeds the hard inventory \(B_i\), then no pathwise-\(B_i\)-feasible policy can exist, because a hard cap \(N_i\le B_i\) on every run implies \(\mathbb E[N_i]\le B_i\). This is a one-way infeasibility test; an expected upper bound below \(B_i\) does **not** prove the hard inventory is never exceeded on rare runs. citeturn707137search2turn458319search2

Dominance in resource-augmented states is exact and especially valuable. For the same abstract crafting state \(s\), a state with resource vector \(b'\ge b\) componentwise weakly dominates \(b\) for maximal success probability because every policy feasible at \(b\) is also feasible at \(b'\). More generally, label-setting algorithms may discard a state/resource label when another label has at least as much remaining resource and no worse certified objective bound. Multi-cost bounded-reachability methods explicitly maintain such Pareto information. citeturn458319search0

The query “this branch cannot finish within inventory with probability above \(q\)” has a particularly clean exact answer: solve or bound the **maximal cost-bounded reachability probability** from that branch. If an optimistic relaxation has maximum success probability below \(q\), prune. If its upper bound is above \(q\), no conclusion follows. Using an optimistic relaxation preserves correctness because removing constraints can only enlarge the policy set and therefore cannot reduce maximal success probability. citeturn458319search0

For very large but rarely binding inventories such as 80,000, exact equality to the unbounded SSP usually cannot be inferred merely because the cap “looks huge”: an unrestricted policy with a geometric repeat loop can have positive probability of using more than every finite \(B\). What can be proved is an **error bound**. Solve the unbounded relaxation for a lower bound on optimal expected cost; construct a budget-feasible policy for an upper bound or certified success probability; if the resulting objective/success gap is below the accepted tolerance, the inventory dimension need not be enumerated further. This fits the existing optimistic/pessimistic abstraction philosophy and avoids arbitrary truncation. citeturn448697search4turn458319search0

There is no need for a hard-coded “100,000 actions is unrealistic” rule. A principled model can charge each operation a second resource—seconds, clicks, or cognitive effort—set a maximum total operation count, or optimize a Pareto front of price versus effort. Expected-total-cost CMDPs support several expected objectives, while hard operation counts are ordinary bounded resources. citeturn707137search0turn458319search0

A second principled option is **satisficing**: require price to be within a user-specified bound, then minimize effort or policy complexity, rather than requiring every decision to be globally price-optimal. A third is to compare restricted “simple” policy classes with the unrestricted optimum and report the certified gap. There is literature on pure/bounded-memory multi-objective strategies, but I did **not** find a general theorem guaranteeing that every finite undiscounted SSP of the type here has a very simple policy within a universal small multiplicative loss. Any such simplicity guarantee should therefore be derived for this problem class, not assumed. citeturn291562search0

## Recommended solver architecture and configuration

The base state should remain the current goal-relevant abstraction. Add a vector \(b\) only for **hard pathwise resources that are actually part of the query**:
\[
x=(s,b_1,\ldots,b_k).
\]
An action consuming vector \(d(a)\) is enabled iff \(d(a)\le b\), and its successors have budget \(b-d(a)\). This turns user inventory into ordinary MDP state and produces exact hard-limit semantics. citeturn458319search0

This structure plugs unusually well into the existing SCC decomposition. Resource-consuming transitions strictly decrease at least one component, so they cannot participate in an SCC across different resource vectors. Only zero-resource actions can create cycles within a fixed budget slice. Thus budget slices can be processed in resource order, with the existing SCC/value- or policy-iteration machinery used inside each slice. This does not remove the potentially huge number of slices, but it converts the resource dimension into a natural dynamic-programming order. citeturn458319search0

The current near-1 self-loop elimination deserves special handling. In the unbounded SSP, replacing repeated failures by a geometric macro with expected cost \(c/(1-p)\) is valid under the appropriate stationary “repeat the same action” semantics. With a hard tracked inventory or hard operation count, each failure decrements the resource and therefore moves to a different augmented state; a single stationary geometric macro would erase decisions the policy may legitimately make when the remaining inventory changes. The safe alternatives are to solve the recurrence between budget layers or to offer a separate exact macro-action meaning “commit to repeat this operation until success or exhaustion.” citeturn458319search0

For expected-only resource constraints, retain the current state space and solve a **CMDP via occupation measures or its Lagrangian dual**. A Lagrangian
\[
c_\lambda(s,a)=c_{\rm money}(s,a)+\sum_i\lambda_i d_i(s,a)
\]
turns a constrained problem into ordinary SSP solves for fixed multipliers, making your highly optimized SSP solver useful as a subroutine. Under the relevant CMDP assumptions, primal/dual LP theory supplies optimality and shadow-price interpretations; randomized stationary policies can be required at the constrained optimum. citeturn707137search2turn458319search2

For each user query, I recommend the following semantic order: determine hard-resource feasibility; if success probability 1 is achievable, minimize expected price subject to the hard limits and any expected constraints; otherwise compute \(P^\*\) and solve MCMP or a user-selected success-floor problem; then, if requested, add exact risk optimization on the surviving graph. This preserves a clear interpretation at every stage and never relies on an arbitrary dead-end penalty. citeturn707137search6turn687284search6

The planner should expose inventories by operation or operation family, a hard money cap, a minimum required success probability, a maximum total operation count, optional hard/expected time or effort, and optional p90/CVaR controls. It should distinguish “hard” from “expected” constraints explicitly because the mathematics and guarantees differ. Chance-constrained or percentile controls should be phrased in probability language, such as “at least 95% probability of spending at most X.” citeturn156635search5turn156635academia9

For diagnostics, report which constraints bind. For continuous expected CMDP constraints, the optimal Lagrange multipliers are genuine marginal/shadow-price information under the duality assumptions. For integer hard budgets, a more honest marginal value is the exact finite difference, e.g. \(V(b)-V(b+e_i)\) or \(P^\*(b+e_i)-P^\*(b)\), because the hard-budget value function can have jumps and need not be concave. citeturn707137search2turn458319search0

A practical default output could therefore read: “success probability 99.83%; expected spend conditional on success 417; unconditional spend 411; p90 486; inventory A is binding; one extra unit of A raises maximal success probability by 0.04 percentage points; money cap is nonbinding; the next non-dominated strategy costs 6 more in expectation and lowers p90 by 21.” Every number in such a report can come from exact policy evaluation, an exact constrained solve, or certified lower/upper bounds rather than simulation. citeturn448697search4turn458319search0

## Practical complexity and what is not established

For the stated target of seconds to roughly one minute on 4–16 cores, the theory supports using hard-budget DP only when the effective resource dimension is very small or heavy dominance collapses the frontier. There is no general algorithmic result that makes several 30,000–80,000-valued independent hard counters cheap; cost-bounded reachability is fundamentally pseudo-polynomial in numeric resource bounds and multi-resource formulations suffer combinatorial growth. citeturn458319search0turn156635academia9

Conversely, Lagrangian/CMDP methods can avoid that numeric-budget blow-up when the user accepts **expected** resource constraints. There are algorithms with value-iteration-like complexity for multiple discounted expected budget constraints, but that specific result is discounted and should not be transferred as a theorem to the undiscounted SSP case. For the undiscounted total-cost setting, Altman’s transient/CMDP LP and duality theory is the appropriate reference. citeturn458319search5turn707137search2

I found no general theorem establishing concavity of hard-budget reachability value in the budget, no exact undiscounted CVaR-SSP implementation demonstrated at \(10^6\) states, and no published result showing exact CVaR optimization on a graph of the R04 scale of roughly \(5.36\times10^8\) transitions. The strongest directly relevant CVaR measurements I found are the AAAI 2022 experiments summarized above, topping out around \(1.4\times10^5\) states and \(3.0\times10^5\) transitions in the reported table. citeturn649738view0

The most promising exact/certified path for this particular planner is therefore **not** “augment every inventory counter and solve the gigantic graph.” It is: use expected-resource LP/Lagrangian relaxations as bounds; detect hard constraints that are provably irrelevant or infeasible; augment only the remaining hard dimensions; exploit resource-layer ordering and Pareto dominance; run the existing lower/upper-bound abstraction machinery on those layers; and reserve distributional-risk optimization for the final reduced state space. Each cut then arises from feasibility, dominance, relaxation bounds, or the user’s own limits rather than a hand-tuned probability or repetition threshold. citeturn707137search2turn458319search0turn448697search0
