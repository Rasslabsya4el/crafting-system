# Prior art and game data sources for a Path of Exile crafting route planner

## Executive findings

The strongest prior art is much closer to your proposed architecture than I expected. **At least three public projects explicitly search over multi-step Path of Exile crafting decisions rather than merely evaluating a fixed recipe**: Denny Britz's 2024 PoE 1 reinforcement-learning/MDP prototype, Walker60's 2026 PoE 2 `POE2Crafting`, and WladHD's 2025–26 PoE 2 `pyoe2-craftpath`. `POE2Crafting` is the closest architectural match: it learns a transition model over compressed states, runs tabular value iteration, outputs a policy, and validates that policy by simulation. `pyoe2-craftpath` instead constructs a pruned transition graph and searches acyclic “sensible” routes, which is useful as a contrasting design because its author explicitly documents where happy-path pruning misses valid strategies. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn161633view1turn161633view2turn161633view3

For PoE 1 specifically, Denny Britz's write-up is the most relevant mathematical precedent I found. It represents an item by target-mod presence plus coarse structural features, learns \(P(s'|s,a)\) by sampling concrete crafts, and solves the resulting MDP with undiscounted Bellman/value iteration. His experience closely matches your state-compression problem: too little state loses Markov information because “junk” mods can alter later pools through tags/families; adding tags and families repairs some of that sim-to-real mismatch. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn161633view1

**Craft of Exile is valuable as an oracle, but I found no evidence that its general calculator is an optimal-policy or general route-search solver.** It has method-specific combinatorial optimizers—notably fossil selection and the 3.24 graveyard optimizer—and built-in multi-stage *methods* such as Alt–Aug–Regal and metacrafting, but these are specialized calculators for a selected method. Its changelog does not document Bellman optimization, MDP solving, or a search over arbitrary action policies. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3

On game data, **RePoE retains several pieces of generation semantics that are easy to lose in a simplified exporter**: ordered `spawn_weights`, ordered `generation_weights`, `adds_tags`, `implicit_tags`, and an array of exclusion `groups`. Its own documentation explicitly says `adds_tags` from a rolled mod are considered when spawning subsequent mods. `[datamined]` citeturn580002view0turn580002view1 This is important enough that I would not treat a PoB export containing only one `group` and a reduced tag representation as authoritative for crafting without diffing it against raw/RePoE data. Current PoB 2.67.2 was released on **August 7, 2026** for the 3.29 cycle; its PoE 1 manifest exposes `ModExplicit.lua`, `ModMaster.lua`, `Essence.lua`, etc., whereas `ModItem.lua` is not the main PoE 1 explicit-mod file in the current manifest. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn958045search0turn958045search1turn324431search0turn324431search1

There are two especially important “do not hard-code as fact” results. First, I found **no GGG primary source or game-data field establishing the familiar rare-item 4/5/6-affix ratio of 8:3:1**; the wiki documents the ratio, and a public simulator repository records an experiment of 4,000 Chaos Orbs on an ilvl-3 Iron Ring specifically to infer that distribution. `[documented]` `[player-experiment]` citeturn908955search0turn908955search2 Second, although the data clearly contain fossil weight multipliers, I did **not** find a primary GGG statement establishing how multiple simultaneous fossil multipliers compose. Community implementations multiply the applicable factors, but under your facts policy that composition rule should remain “datamined/tool-implementation corroborated, primary proof not found” until independently tested or located in executable/game code. `[datamined]` `[tool-implementation]` citeturn324431search4turn908955search3

A patch-data warning is also warranted. The legacy Craft of Exile changelog says its last PoE 1 dataset update was **July 27, 2026, to 3.29.0.3**, while its current beta identifies the latest PoE 1 data as **3.29.3.1.2**. PoB 2.67.2 was released August 7. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3turn958045search2turn958045search1 The public 3.29.3 notes include some crafting-adjacent fixes, such as a bench-recipe-description correction and an Essence-drop bug, but I found no primary note in the searched material announcing a general affix-generation algorithm change after 3.29.0.3. `[official]` citeturn958045search3 That is not proof that no data changed; a raw table diff should be part of every patch ingestion.

## Craft of Exile in depth

### Architecture and calculation model

Craft of Exile exposes three distinct user-facing ideas—**Calculator, Simulator and Emulator**—and its current application metadata still enumerates those as separate modules. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0turn685918view1 The beta site identifies separate Web Worker bundles for the calculator, simulator and fossil optimizer, which confirms that significant probability work is performed client-side. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn958045search2

The legacy calculator is primarily an **analytical combinatorial evaluator** of a selected crafting method. Its UI dynamically creates one to three affix-selection columns depending on the selected method; for example, its Alt–Aug–Regal calculator considers valid three-affix combinations and can mark one affix mandatory. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0 The changelog documents an “average spending per success” calculation added March 10, 2020, and later method-specific additions such as Alt–Aug, Alt–Aug–Regal, metacrafting and automatic best-fossil selection. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3

The **Simulator** is the repeated-trial side of the system, while the **Emulator** applies concrete crafting actions to an item interactively; the changelog repeatedly distinguishes fixes that affected calculator, simulator and emulator separately. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 This distinction is useful for your architecture: an exact transition kernel, a Monte Carlo validator, and an interactive concrete-item executor should be separable modules rather than three modes of one opaque function.

The user-provided internal names such as `poec_computeFinalProbsV2`, `poec_computePermutationsV2` and `poec_simRecombinate` are consistent with the site's client-side architecture, but **I did not find a public source repository or author-written mathematical reference documenting those specific functions**, so I would not treat their implementation details as a citable specification. `[tool-implementation]` The public changelog is the strongest documentation I found for algorithm changes. citeturn853916view3

### Data provenance and patch updates

The legacy site says its last PoE 1 game-data refresh was **July 27, 2026 to patch 3.29.0.3**. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 The newer beta currently advertises **3.29.3.1.2** and exposes older 3.29 snapshots in its patch selector. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn958045search2 This matters for oracle use: for 3.29 testing, the beta—not the legacy calculator snapshot—is the better comparison target.

For PoE 2, Craft of Exile explicitly credits **Krakenbul / Prohibited Library** for weighting data and `pathofexile-dat` for extraction/viewing. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view1 The retrieved public About page does not give an equivalently explicit current PoE 1 raw-data provenance statement. Its historical behavior and raw-export functionality show that it consumes extracted game data, but I did not find a public build pipeline documenting exactly which DAT files become each PoE 1 client dataset. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3

Its update process is visibly **patch-snapshot based**: the changelog repeatedly records data updates—3.10, 3.11, 3.13, 3.14, 3.17, 3.20 through 3.29—alongside hand-written mechanic corrections. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 That separation is crucial: “new DAT dump” and “crafting semantics changed” are not the same operation.

### Known disagreements and acknowledged assumptions

Craft of Exile is unusually useful because its own FAQ documents places where the author **does not claim certainty**.

For **Sanctified Fossil**, the site says its formula was extrapolated from a 1,000-result in-game dataset generated by Sectoidfodder and explicitly says an assumption remains. `[player-experiment]` `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0 That is exactly the epistemic discipline your facts policy calls for.

For the historical Harvest “reforge more likely” mechanic, the FAQ says the author was told the old data had contained a **90×** multiplier and that it was later removed from the game files; the implementation therefore multiplies weights of existing mods by 90. `[datamined]` `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0 This is not equivalent to a current primary GGG guarantee.

For historical “reforge more common” Harvest crafts, the FAQ is even more explicit: the site **assumed** they worked like fossils and used a 10× matching-tag multiplier, while acknowledging the real value could differ. `[speculation]` `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0 This number should not be imported into a verified-mechanics model.

The changelog records repeated mismatches involving metamods and Harvest. On **July 30, 2021**, it says Harvest reforges again did not respect Cannot Roll Attack/Caster; other entries alter whether particular rerolls respect locks, and the Dec. 30, 2021 entry changes Annul behavior to account for those metamods to match live behavior. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 These are valuable regression-test dates.

The changelog also records incomplete Recombinator support: on **June 4, 2022** it added recombination but stated that “tier change” was still unfinished. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 Therefore a historical Craft of Exile Recombinator result from that period cannot be taken as a full specification of the live Sentinel-era algorithm.

For fossils, the site has repeatedly changed its behavior and optimizer, including a September 2020 fossil/tag adjustment and an April 2020 rewrite of the fossil optimizer to use the normal calculation algorithm. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 I did not find an author publication proving the mathematical composition rule for overlapping fossil modifiers from first principles.

The site's public disclaimer/FAQ also warns that results are not infallible and uses mass simulation as a cross-check for difficult/skewed pools. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0 That makes it appropriate as an **independent oracle**, not as ground truth.

### Route optimization: what it does and does not do

Craft of Exile certainly contains **optimization**, but the scope matters. It added an automatic “best fossil selection” suggestion in April 2020, then a graveyard optimizer in April 2024, including a “true optimizer” that runs corpse assignments through the calculator. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 These optimize parameters *inside a chosen crafting mechanism*.

It also contains composite methods such as Alt–Aug–Regal, Alt–Aug–Imprint–Regal, metacrafting and simulator “meta-methods” such as wiping a side or annul-saving a side. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 These are predefined procedure templates.

I found **no public evidence of a general action-space search that computes an optimal adaptive policy over arbitrary item states**. `[tool-implementation]` Its public documentation describes calculators and method-specific optimizers, not a Bellman value function or a policy mapping every reachable state to an action. citeturn685918view0turn853916view3 That difference is substantive: a method calculator can answer “what does this selected procedure cost per success?”, while your solver needs to answer “after this particular miss, should I annul, reroll, lock, sell, or restart?”

### Reuse and licensing

I found **no published open-source licence for Craft of Exile's client code or datasets** on the public About/FAQ/changelog pages examined. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn685918view0turn685918view1turn853916view3 Accordingly, its code should be treated as inspectable behavior, not reusable source, unless the author supplies permission elsewhere. This matches your stated plan to use it only as an oracle.

Its public documentation is, however, highly reusable as a **test catalogue**: dates of mechanic fixes, acknowledged assumptions, patch transitions, and method-specific probability outputs can all become black-box regression cases without copying implementation. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3turn685918view0

### Write-ups treating crafting/upgrading as an MDP or optimal control problem

**Denny Britz, “Solving Path of Exile item crafting with Reinforcement Learning,” July 13, 2024.** `[tool-implementation]` The article formulates crafting as an MDP, constructs an empirical table \(P(s'|s,a)\), and solves it with the Bellman equation using Q-value iteration and \(\gamma=1\). It explicitly suggests exact distributions where tractable and sampling only where the transition outcome set is too large. citeturn161633view1 The most transferable result is not merely “RL works”; it is that **model-based dynamic programming is fast once a useful compressed model exists**, and that state aliases can prevent repeatedly sampling equivalent state-action pairs. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn161633view1

Britz also demonstrates objective sensitivity. A policy minimizing crafting steps can differ sharply from one minimizing currency cost, so “expected number of actions,” “expected chaos-equivalent cost,” and “success probability under budget” should be separate objective functions rather than a single heuristic score. `[tool-implementation]` This is directly analogous to your expected-cost versus within-budget-success modes. citeturn853916view0

**Lost Ark `facet` technical note.** `[tool-implementation]` The repository gives an exact finite-horizon MDP/expectimax recursion and a quantitative state-space bound. Because all paths into an equivalent count-state have the same future, it memoizes state values rather than histories. It also uses exact fixed-width integer arithmetic for probability comparisons, avoiding floating-point instability in deciding the optimal action. citeturn161633view0 For PoE, exact rational arithmetic is probably too expensive globally, but the design suggests a good validation technique: use high-precision or rational arithmetic on tiny reduced pools to test Bellman decisions.

**Additive-discovery / recombination-game research.** `[documented]` I found academic work modeling innovation games in which agents spend finite resources either exploiting existing discoveries or attempting stochastic recombination/discovery; these are finite-state decision problems and produce threshold or “switch-once” policies under particular assumptions. The mathematical analogy is real—stochastic resource-consuming action, state-dependent future opportunities and an optimal stopping/switch point—but the state dynamics are much simpler than PoE and do not model mutually exclusive affix families or reroll actions. `[documented]` The principal transferable idea is structural policy analysis: when monotonicity exists, one can sometimes prove that only a restricted class of actions needs consideration. For PoE, such pruning should be proved action-by-action rather than assumed globally.

I did **not** find a peer-reviewed paper that models a modern ARPG affix-crafting system with anything close to PoE's combination of state-dependent weighted pools, locks, rerolls, removal, influence and restart loops. `[documented]` The best directly applicable mathematical sources remain the PoE-specific Britz implementation and finite-policy game solvers such as `facet`.

### Added tags and subsequent draws

**Answer to (a): yes.** A tag added by one modifier can change the spawn weights/eligibility of modifiers drawn later on the same item. `[datamined]` RePoE's `mods.json` documentation says `adds_tags` are added to an item when the mod is present and are “taken into consideration when spawning other mods,” specifically through `spawn_weights` and `generation_weights`. citeturn580002view0

This is not just prose documentation. RePoE's pool-generation code starts with base tags, repeatedly scans modifiers, and whenever a mod has an `adds_tags` value not already in the conditional tag set, adds it and restarts the scan; modifiers whose weight changes under the enlarged tag set are marked conditional. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn580002view3

The model consequence is severe: **“target set + number of junk prefixes/suffixes” is not always Markov** unless the compressed state also captures the effective tag closure induced by those junk modifiers. `[speculation]` Two states with the same target bits and junk counts can have different future transition kernels if one junk mod contributes an `adds_tags` tag.

### Cannot Roll Attack / Cannot Roll Caster

**Answer to (b): the blocking is encoded through special item-state tags in spawn weights, not by checking a candidate mod's `adds_tags`.** `[datamined]` RePoE gives a concrete example: `AddedColdDamage1` has `no_attack_mods` with weight 0; when the item carries the corresponding metamod state, that special tag becomes the first matching spawn-weight entry and the attack mod becomes ineligible. citeturn580002view0

This should be distinguished from **crafting classification tags** such as Attack or Caster. RePoE's `implicit_tags` are the tags used by crafting methods such as fossils and Harvest. `[datamined]` citeturn580002view0 The actual metamod exclusion is represented in the target mod's ordered `spawn_weights` by entries such as `no_attack_mods` or the corresponding caster sentinel. `[datamined]` citeturn580002view0

Historical behavior has been tricky enough that third-party tools repeatedly changed it. Craft of Exile fixed its attack/caster flag mapping on **April 12, 2020**, later altered Annul behavior on **December 30, 2021**, and documented periods when Harvest reforges did not respect those metamods. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn853916view3 A 2020 GGG forum bug-report thread also illustrates why “has a caster-looking tag in a third-party display” was not by itself a reliable test of metamod behavior. `[player-experiment]` citeturn958045search4

For implementation, the safest rule is therefore: **evaluate the actual ordered spawn-weight conditions after applying the metamod's item-state tag**, rather than writing `if "attack" in mod.tags: block`. `[speculation]` That mirrors the datamined representation and automatically covers exceptions.

### Mod groups and exclusion families

**Answer to (c): the underlying game-data representation can contain several exclusion groups/families; a single scalar `group` should not be assumed complete.** `[datamined]` RePoE exports `groups` as an array and states that for normally spawnable mods, one mod from a group prevents another member of that group from appearing simultaneously. citeturn580002view2

Accordingly, if a PoB-generated record exposes only one `group`, **you must verify how its exporter derived that value before using it as the game's complete conflict relation**. `[speculation]` The current PoB PoE1 data loader confirms that PoB consumes generated modifier tables for build/item use, but the retrieved source does not establish a guarantee that all raw family memberships survive as a multi-valued exclusion set. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn324431search1

This is a case where RePoE/raw DAT should be used to build an explicit `mod_id -> frozenset(family_ids)` relation. `[speculation]` Then conflict is an intersection test, not string equality on one `group`.

### Rare affix-count distribution

**Answer to (d): I did not find a primary GGG or datamined source for the 4/5/6 weights 8/3/1.** The PoE Wiki currently documents that regular Chaos-Orb rares have 4, 5 or 6 affixes in an **8:3:1** ratio. `[documented]` citeturn908955search0

A concrete public experimental source exists: Gloorf's simulator repository says its `data/ironring_level3` dataset came from **4,000 Chaos Orb rolls on an ilvl-3 Iron Ring**, specifically to determine the proportion of four-, five- and six-mod results. `[player-experiment]` citeturn908955search2 That gives a defensible empirical provenance, but it is not GGG primary evidence.

PoEDB documents only the weaker rule that a normal item made rare by Chance/Alchemy has at least four affixes and that Chaos rerolls a rare to between four and six modifiers; the retrieved page does not provide the 8:3:1 probabilities. `[datamined]` citeturn908955search1

Under your facts policy, I would encode this as something like:

`rare_affix_count_distribution = {4: 8/12, 5: 3/12, 6: 1/12, provenance="community experiment/wiki; no primary source found"}`. `[speculation]`

It is a perfect candidate for a fresh automated live-game experiment, with confidence intervals, because a small shift in these probabilities affects every Chaos/Alchemy transition.

### Multiple fossil weight modifiers

**Answer to (e): the extracted data clearly give each fossil independent positive/negative tag-weight modifiers, but I did not find primary GGG evidence in the searched sources proving the combination operator for two fossils affecting the same mod.** `[datamined]` A Serrated Fossil, for example, is exported with a positive Attack modifier and negative Caster modifier in RePoE's `fossils.json`. citeturn324431search4

## Recommended architecture and the five things to reuse

### Policy iteration/value iteration over a deliberately sufficient compressed state

**Rank 1 — copy the architecture, not the code, of Britz and `POE2Crafting`.** `[tool-implementation]` Both separate “what happens when I take action \(a\) in state \(s\)” from “which action should I choose?”, then solve the latter with Bellman dynamic programming. citeturn161633view1turn161633view2 This is a much better fit than enumerating craft recipes because PoE naturally has recovery loops: annul and retry, lock and scour, sell/rebuy, or restart. Your stochastic-shortest-path framing is therefore already aligned with the strongest prior art. The key refinement is to make state equivalence a testable contract: two concrete states may share one compressed state only if every supported action produces the same compressed transition distribution and immediate cost. `[speculation]`

### RePoE/raw-data semantics for generation, with PoB as a cross-check

**Rank 2 — copy RePoE's semantic preservation of ordered weights, added tags and multi-group exclusion.** `[datamined]` Its documentation exposes exactly the fields that matter for a generative simulator: ordered `spawn_weights`, `generation_weights`, `adds_tags`, `implicit_tags` and `groups`. citeturn580002view0turn580002view1turn580002view2 PoB 2.67.2 is extremely useful and current for 3.29-era item data, but its present PoE1 data layout is oriented toward PoB's own modifier model. `[tool-implementation]` citeturn958045search1turn324431search1 I would ingest raw/RePoE IDs and causal fields first, then attach PoB's cleaned tiers/text/ranges where convenient. That avoids discovering later that a supposedly irrelevant “junk” modifier silently changed the next roll's pool.

### Reachable-state construction plus exact/sampled hybrid transitions

**Rank 3 — reuse Britz's “exact where small, sample where huge” idea and `POE2Crafting`'s model/replay split.** `[tool-implementation]` Britz explicitly notes that there is no reason to Monte Carlo a transition whose complete outcome distribution is tractable; sampling should be reserved for oversized distributions. citeturn161633view1 That maps neatly to your action set. Annulment, deterministic bench crafts, many Exalt-like single draws, Fracture from a small explicit set, and several Eldritch operations can often be enumerated exactly from the compressed state, whereas full Chaos/Alchemy/Fossil rerolls may benefit from a specialized convolution/enumerator or sampled approximation. `[speculation]` Use the concrete simulator only as a validator of the compressed transition kernel; do not make policy evaluation itself dependent on noisy Monte Carlo if exact transitions are available.

### Explicit provenance and uncertainty as part of the mechanics database

**Rank 4 — copy Craft of Exile's willingness to label assumptions, but formalize it.** `[tool-implementation]` Its FAQ openly says when a Sanctified formula came from 1,000 experimental samples and when a Harvest multiplier was an extrapolation. citeturn685918view0 Your rules database should make that machine-readable: `source_kind`, source URL, patch introduced, patch last verified, confidence/status, and optionally a test fixture. `[speculation]` Then the solver can refuse to use actions whose mechanics are below a requested evidence threshold. The 8:3:1 rare-affix distribution and multi-fossil composition are immediate examples where provenance should survive all the way to the displayed policy rather than disappearing inside a constant.

### Full-policy output plus distributional cost evaluation

**Rank 5 — combine the `facet` compact-policy idea with a second pass that computes the entire cost distribution under the chosen policy.** `[tool-implementation]` `facet` shows that once the state is compressed, the optimal decision itself can be tiny relative to the full search tree. citeturn161633view0 For your output, save `policy[state] -> action`, then analyze the induced Markov chain separately to obtain expected cost, success probability under a budget, and p90 cost. `[speculation]` This separation is important because optimizing expectation does **not** automatically optimize the 90th percentile or probability of completion under a hard budget. A budget-aware version can augment state with remaining budget or solve a finite-horizon/resource-constrained MDP; the expected-cost SSP can stay much smaller. `[speculation]` It also gives you a clean way to explain routes: render the policy as loops and recovery branches for humans, while retaining the state-action table as the actual solver output.
